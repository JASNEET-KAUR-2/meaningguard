**Discussion.** The main MuRIL multi-task model reaches 99.2% binary accuracy (macro-F1 0.991) with 98.6% negation recall and a 1.4% false-positive rate on the test split. Two observations matter. First, the training data is synthetic and rule-generated: preserving pairs are typically transliterations (script differs, meaning identical), while altering pairs differ by exactly one edit operation (a deleted `mat`, a swapped numeral, an entity swap). The model learns this signal reliably and, in effect, memorises the *surface signature* of the five edit families rather than semantic equivalence. This is why accuracy is near-ceiling on the benchmark but should not be extrapolated to real rewrites. Second, the ablation results confirm the design hypothesis only weakly: A1 (binary head only) drops macro-F1 from 0.991 to 0.971 and negation recall from 98.6% to 96.4%, so the dimension head contributes modestly. A3 shows that the choice of encoder matters more than task formulation: XLM-R (0.963) and IndicBERT (0.974) trail MuRIL (0.991) despite the same head architecture, and MuRIL's pre-training on transliterated Indian-language text plausibly explains the gap. The comparably strong translation-pipeline baseline (95.0% accuracy) on the same test set underscores that naive normalisation catches most synthetic edits; the meaningful deltas are the residual 5% of cases involving spelling variants (`please`/`kripya`) where the pipeline fails but MuRIL, trained on such variants, does not.

The 112 misclassifications of the trained model cluster into three interpretable families. (1) **Intent–negation confusion.** When a sentence is rewritten from imperative (`भरो`, "fill!") to an interrogative form (`भरना है?`, "to fill?"), the model labels the difference as negation instead of intent, because both transformations lengthen the sentence around the verb. Example: `प्लीज़ फॉर्म भरना है?` vs `प्लीज़ फॉर्म भरो` was labelled `negation` with confidence 0.61 despite the absence of any negation marker. (2) **Spelling variants without signal.** Cases where the only edit is a lexical variant (`please` ↔ `kripya`, `Swiggy` ↔ `swiggy`) are occasionally flagged as changed, inflating the false-positive rate. These are precisely the cases the training pipeline treats as preserved, which is why the overall FPR stays low (1.4%) but not zero. (3) **Morphological number changes.** Swapping `ek` (one) to `do` (two) inside a longer sentence occasionally slips through because the numeral is short and the surrounding context dominates the representation; the model flags the sentence as preserved when the quantity actually changed. All three failure modes share a structural cause: the rule-based generator produced contrast pairs where the edit is lexically small, so the model relies on token-level signals rather than compositional semantics. Real-world rewrites — where edits span multiple tokens and change phrasing — would surface more of these failure modes.
